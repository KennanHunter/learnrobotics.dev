# How strong is AI really

I still remember the day vividly when I read Deepseek's paper on their R1
model.<Citation

              description="DeepSeek's 2025 paper introducing the R1 reasoning model."

going through the different colors to find the ones that she liked. A coworker sent me the paper
for me to read.

They weren't the first model to exhibit reasoning behavior, but it was the first time
I saw proof that these models could really think, or at least, do something close to it.
Prior to that I and I think many other developers just saw them as fancy autocomplete.

**AI is at the point that it could very easily completely write an elite level
robot codebase from start to finish in about an hour.**

Certain frontier models have proved themselves very capable in certain domains. In math,
OpenAI published 100s of conjectures,<Citation href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI_bulk_release_of_mathematical_papers" title="OpenAI bulk release of mathematical papers" description="Wikipedia summary of the 2025 release of AI-assisted mathematical conjectures and proofs." />

and AI images and writing is everywhere.

AI in professional software development at the moment is everywhere. According to JetBrains
research in August 2026 <Citation href="https://blog.jetbrains.com/research/2026/08/how-much-code-do-developers-really-let-agents-write/" title="How Much Code Do Developers Really Let Agents Write?" description="A survey on AI agent usage" />

> ~47% of their code is fully written by agents.
>

>

If I'm being honest, I think that these numbers are still a little bit low, and in newer,
smaller projects, and projects that work as internal tools, the number is much higher.

I don't think AI is going anywhere. I think that models will only get better. I think that
there are a lot of economic factors that will lead to AI being used more and more in various
domains.

# Why for FIRST I'm still skeptical

## Internet access

Without internet, most models are unusable. Open source models exist, but on common
hardware they're pretty limited. Most FIRST competitions I've been to have had no or
poor internet connectivity, where AI models will be pretty much useless.

## Lack of understanding of your robot

No two robots are the exact same. A model can work well with what exists in it's context such as
code, documentation, or system diagrams. When an encoder wire comes loose and you have to figure
out if it's because of an electrical issue or an issue in multiple layers of software,
I'm not sure that AI will be able to help. AI will probably never be very good at watching a robot
on the field and recognizing that its PID needs a stronger P parameter.

## Judging

I've always thought that judging is underutilized in FIRST programming. Because judges typically
have limited programming experience, and I've never seen a judge look directly at code, they
tend to not judge many aspects I would consider important, such as code cleanliness and
optimization. Regardless, communicating coding concepts succinctly to judges is an important part
of many teams strategies, and I think that hand writing some, most, if not all, of your code
will help you convey the concepts you're using better.

## Spaghetti

Left unchecked, AI models can code themselves into a ever deepening bowl of spaghetti code,
where a single structural issue is fixed with a band-aid fix, then the issues caused by that
band-aid warrant another band-aid. This is bad, especially when you don't have the experience
to recognize a band-aid.

## Where it works

Where it works is still probably going to be one off applications. Things like scouting apps,
student tracking, internal tools. Read [4414 HighTide's post on their tide apps](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/team-4414-hightide-2026-tech-binder-ripcurrent/519602/75)
for more information on a very good teams thoughts on the subject.

# Why programming is important even with AI

## Verification

For the near future, being able to read and verify AI generated code will continue to
be a very useful and in demand skill. AI can insert security issues, data privacy issues,
and just generally can't know everything about the problem space you're working in, especially
if you're working on particularly difficult issues.

## "in distribution"

You'll sometimes hear "techies" on places like Twitter refer to certain problems as
"in distribution", what they mean by that is that the problem has been solved before, and models
have been trained on similar solutions before. Your code that takes peoples cat pics and adds
a top hat? Probably in distribution. Firmware for your custom operating system?
Probably not, and AI models will have a tougher time at the moment. Nobody can say how long that
will be true.

## Systems thinking

>  “Underlying our approach to this subject is our conviction that 'computer science' is
> not a science and that its significance has little to do with computers. The computer
> revolution is a revolution in the way we think and in the way we express what we think.

> epistemology—the study of the structure of knowledge from an imperative point of view,
> as opposed to the more declarative point of view taken by classical mathematical subjects.

Programming is the act of formalizing thinking. You take a complicated process, and you
break it down into smaller problems. Those smaller problems themselves have problems
inside them to be solved. That skill will always be useful.

# The skeptics

So far I've given a lot of credence to the idea that AI programming is going to be a meaningful
game changer. I truly think it is. But there are plenty of very smart people who would disagree
with me.

> When people see an artifact, they make assumptions about the process that was used to create it.
> Without even thinking about it, they assume the creator had a basically human state of mind.
> This assumption is no longer true. Things can be broken in ways that weren’t previously possible,
> and old proxies of underlying quality like syntax and grammar are useless. AI produced artifacts
> are not produced by the same process as human ones, and this difference, while extremely subtle
> in statistics, makes itself obvious when you try to interact with and build on the artifact in human ways.
> - George Hotz, [Comma.AI founder](https://comma.ai/) and [edgy soundcloud rapper (Warning: Profanity)](https://soundcloud.com/tomcr00se/tracks)

# The joy of programming

There are a lot of reasons people program.

1. You want to make something work.
1. You want to be rewarded, with authority, money, or recognition.
1. You love building something that’s really yours, something you
   understand well enough to share.

There’s nothing wrong with the first two. Honestly, long term, they’re
probably the most stable reasons to keep learning, keep trying, keep going
when it’s 10pm, your robot has to drive at a competition tomorrow, and you
know you’re the only one who can fix it.

But I think the third one matters too.

When you’re at a competition and someone from another team comes up and
asks how you programmed your auto, you want to be able to explain what you
did. When I was a student, there was no better feeling than connecting with
another student and talking about everything we went through and everything
we learned. Some of my strongest friendships started that way: learning
about what each other was working on, cracking in-jokes only programmers
would get, and reaching out to each other for help, advice, or just a second
set of eyes.

I’m not saying AI takes all of that away, and I’m sure there are people who
“vibecode” robots and still get the same experiences I did. But I think there’s
a lot of value in knowing what you built inside and out.

At the end of your season, when the competitions are over and you’ve felt all
the highs and lows, you’re left with a robot, a codebase, and memories. Do you
want that codebase to be the raw output of an LLM that nobody understands? Or
do you want it to tell a story?

And for a lot of students reading this, I probably sound crazy. When you
program, it’s hours of “why doesn’t this work?” followed by more hours
of “wait, why does this work?” That frustration kind of sucks for a while,
and I won’t pretend the software world is all sunshine and rainbows. I've programmed
nearly daily for 8 years now, and there are still times I need to get up, drink some
water, and take a walk to get back on track. But I bet that at some point, after you’ve
been debugging something for three hours and finally figure it out, you’ll feel a
genuine euphoria that will make it all seem worth it.

The goal of these courses isn’t to remove the frustration. It’s to guide you through it,
so that when you’re at a competition with no internet and no LLM, you get one hour of
frustration instead of three.
